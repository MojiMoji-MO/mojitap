Using Visual Cues on SaaS Pricing Pages Without Creating Confusion

Written by Bavum, Builder and Editor at Mojitap · Originally published July 2026 · Updated July 27, 2026 · About 8 minutes

A pricing page is a decision tool. Its primary job is to help a visitor compare plans, understand the total cost, and identify what happens after purchase. Visual cues can improve scanning, but they should not make one plan look cheaper than it is or hide an important limitation.

Pricing-page essentials

  • Clear billing period and currency
  • Visible included and excluded features
  • Understandable usage limits
  • Accurate trial, renewal, and cancellation terms
  • A comparison that remains usable without color or emoji

1. Use check marks only for real inclusions

Check marks are useful when plan rows are short and comparable. Each row still needs a specific label. “Advanced reporting” is clearer when the page explains what reports are included. Do not use a check beside a feature that requires a separate payment, add-on, higher usage tier, or manual approval without stating the condition.

2. Show exclusions and limits plainly

A dash, cross, or lock can indicate that a feature is unavailable, but the meaning should be explained. Avoid low-contrast exclusions or tooltips that contain essential pricing information. Mobile users and keyboard users must be able to reach the same details.

3. Explain why a plan is recommended

A star or “recommended” label should correspond to an understandable use case, not merely direct attention to the most expensive option. Write “Best for teams that need SSO and audit logs” instead of relying on a crown or flame. The visitor can then judge whether the recommendation applies.

4. Avoid false urgency and preselected consent

Timers, warning symbols, and highlighted buttons should not create a deadline that does not exist. Optional add-ons should not be preselected without clear disclosure. Regulators use the term dark patterns for interface practices that can trick or trap consumers. A pricing page should help a buyer compare, not make an unintended choice more likely.

5. Design for narrow screens

Large comparison tables often fail on mobile. Present the plan name, price, billing period, and primary limits before secondary details. Allow horizontal scrolling only when necessary, preserve row labels, and avoid placing critical terms in hover-only tooltips.

6. Test comprehension before conversion

  1. Ask a new user to identify the total monthly or annual charge.
  2. Ask which limits apply to the chosen plan.
  3. Ask what happens when the trial ends.
  4. Ask how to cancel or change plans.
  5. Fix unclear answers before testing decorative visual changes.

Team — $29 per month

✅ Up to 10 members

✅ Email support

— SSO not included

Renews monthly until canceled. Taxes may apply.

7. Use Mojitap for microcopy, not pricing decisions

Mojitap can suggest an optional icon for a feature label, warning, or confirmation. It does not determine whether terms are legally sufficient, whether a plan is fairly priced, or which option a customer should choose. Review every label with product, support, finance, and legal stakeholders as appropriate.

Official reference

  • Federal Trade Commission: Dark patterns designed to trick or trap consumers

Pricing-page check: Hide the visual cues and compare the plans again. Names, prices, billing periods, limits, exclusions, and the selected action should remain understandable without color or emoji. Restore only the cues that help comparison without steering users through ambiguity.
