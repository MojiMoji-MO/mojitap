How to Test Emoji and No-Emoji Variants Without Misreading Results

Written by Bavum, Builder and Editor at Mojitap · Originally published June 2026 · Updated September 16, 2026

To compare an emoji version with a text-only version, keep the message and delivery conditions as similar as the experiment allows. Decide what outcome matters before looking at results, use a consistent denominator, and distinguish an observed difference from evidence that the emoji caused it. A larger percentage is not automatically a reliable improvement.

This is a planning and arithmetic worksheet. Every count below is invented for explanation; none is a Mojitap customer result. It will help you avoid common interpretation errors, but it does not replace the statistical design or reporting method of your testing platform.

Write the comparison so only the emoji changes

A: Your pottery workshop starts Saturday

B: Your pottery workshop starts Saturday 🏺

Not the same comparison: 🚨 Last chance! Reserve your pottery class now!

A and B differ by one optional symbol. The last example changes urgency, wording, and the requested action. A different result would not isolate the effect of an emoji.

Keep the sender, audience rules, destination, timing, and measurement window aligned. For a product screen, also keep the behavior of the button and the task unchanged. Do not describe a switch from last week's subject to this week's new subject as a randomized experiment.

Define the outcome and denominator together

“More engagement” is too vague to calculate. Choose an outcome you can observe and define who had a chance to produce it. For example, an email experiment might count unique recipients who clicked the relevant link divided by delivered messages. A product experiment might count assigned users who completed the task divided by eligible users exposed to the variant.

QuestionPossible primary outcomeImportant check
Does the subject help the intended recipient act?Relevant unique clicks per delivered emailDo not mix total clicks with unique recipients.
Does a status label help complete the next task?Users completing that task per exposed userUse the same eligibility and observation window.
Does a notification lead to the promised action?Users completing that action per assigned eligible userKeep delivery failures and denominator rules visible.

The correct measure depends on the question. A high open count cannot establish that a task became easier, and repeated clicks from one person should not be mistaken for many people succeeding.

Worked example: 12 clicks versus 16 clicks

Suppose each version reaches 2,000 recipients under the same measurement definition.

A: 12 ÷ 2,000 × 100 = 0.6%

B: 16 ÷ 2,000 × 100 = 0.8%

Absolute difference: 0.8% − 0.6% = 0.2 percentage points.

Relative difference: (0.8% − 0.6%) ÷ 0.6% ≈ 33.3%.

“A 33.3% relative increase” sounds large, but here it describes four additional observed clicks. The arithmetic alone says nothing about random variation, assignment quality, or whether another run would produce the same direction.

A responsible summary is: “The emoji variant recorded 16 clicks compared with 12 for the text-only variant, with 2,000 recipients in each group. We have not established a reliable effect.” Use the experiment tool's uncertainty estimates and design assumptions before making a stronger claim.

Worked example: more clicks can mean a lower rate

Now suppose A records 20 clicks from 1,000 recipients and B records 30 from 3,000. A has a 2% rate; B has a 1% rate. Choosing B because 30 is greater than 20 ignores the different opportunities to click.

This does not automatically prove A is the better variant either. First ask why group sizes differ. Was the planned allocation unequal, or did eligibility, delivery, or measurement fail differently? Record both counts and rates before interpreting the outcome.

Decide how the experiment ends before watching the chart

Write down the assignment method, primary metric, observation window, and stopping rule. Some tools support sequential methods; others expect a fixed design. Follow the method your tool actually implements rather than borrowing a rule from an unrelated product.

Firebase's A/B testing documentation, for example, distinguishes observed results from inference and reports confidence intervals and p-values. Those outputs answer more than the arithmetic rate comparison above. Their meaning depends on that product's statistical setup; they are not evidence that emoji work universally.

Do not stop simply when one result first looks favorable, extend only an unfavorable test, or search many secondary outcomes for the one positive number. Such choices change how convincing the result is. Record deviations instead of hiding them in a success summary.

Use this record when a result is inconclusive

Question: Does one pottery emoji change relevant link clicks?

Unchanged elements: Subject wording, sender, destination, audience eligibility.

Changed element: One emoji at the end of the subject.

Observed counts: Record numerator and denominator for both groups.

Uncertainty: Record the platform's method and interval, or state that inference was not performed.

Decision: Keep the clearer or simpler version when the evidence does not justify a performance claim.

An inconclusive experiment still tells you not to advertise a universal uplift. You can select the text-only version because it is easier to maintain or the emoji version because it fits a documented style, while being honest that the test did not establish a performance benefit.
