Slack and Discord Emojis: Messages, Reactions, and Custom Emoji

Written by Bavum, Builder and Editor at Mojitap · Originally published July 2026 · Updated August 7, 2026

Slack and Discord use emojis in several different ways: as characters inside a message, as reactions attached to a message, and as workspace- or server-specific custom emoji. Choose the feature that matches the action, and keep important status, ownership, and deadlines in text.

Feature rule

Use a reaction for a lightweight response, a message emoji for tone or structure, and a custom emoji only when the community already understands it. Do not make a custom symbol the only way to learn a task's status.

Distinguish the available formats

Custom emojiAvailable within a workspace or server under a custom nameLocal vocabulary understood by that community.
Sticker or imageSent as richer visual contentExpression; it may require descriptive text when meaning matters.

Use reactions for responses, not hidden decisions

Slack notes that reactions can replace a short follow-up message. That works for acknowledgement or a familiar low-risk convention. A production approval, payment authorization, legal decision, or deadline change should also be recorded in words so the decision is searchable and unambiguous.

Explain custom emoji conventions

A custom emoji name may be meaningful only inside one workspace or server. Document recurring conventions in a channel topic, onboarding note, or team guide. Avoid assigning several meanings to the same custom symbol.

Expect permissions and availability to differ

Slack and Discord provide controls for custom emoji, and the available actions depend on workspace, server, role, subscription, and administrator settings. If a custom emoji is missing, first determine whether it belongs to the current workspace or server and whether the account has permission to use or manage it.

Make channels and messages searchable

Do not begin every announcement with an unrelated custom emoji and omit the topic. Put the product area, incident, event, or action in searchable words. Use threads to keep follow-up details connected, and edit the original message when the official status changes.

A practical review

  1. Identify whether the intended action is a message, reaction, custom emoji, or sticker.
  2. Write the status, owner, timing, and action in words.
  3. Use one consistent cue for a recurring state.
  4. Check that new members can understand the message without knowing local emoji slang.
  5. Replace custom imagery with a standard emoji or text when the message must travel outside the community.
