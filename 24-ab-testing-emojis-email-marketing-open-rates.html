How to A/B Test Emojis in Email Subject Lines

Written by Bavum, Builder and Editor at Mojitap · Originally published July 2026 · Updated July 25, 2026 · About 8 minutes

An emoji in an email subject line is a variable, not a strategy by itself. It may help a message stand out for one audience and make another message feel less professional. The only reliable way to learn is to compare controlled versions while protecting sender reputation and keeping the subject truthful.

Simple test design

Version A: Your July account summary

Version B: Your July account summary 📊

Keep the sender, offer, preview text, content, and send time the same.

1. Test one question at a time

If the emoji version also changes the wording, sender name, capitalization, and preview text, the result cannot tell you which change mattered. Start with one practical question: does one relevant emoji improve or weaken this specific subject line for this audience?

2. Create equivalent versions

Both subject lines must make the same honest promise. Do not compare a neutral subject with an exaggerated version and then attribute the difference to the emoji. Avoid fake reply prefixes, false urgency, or symbols that imitate a verified badge, security notice, or operating-system alert.

3. Choose the audience before the winner rule

Test people who are eligible to receive the same campaign and have a similar relationship with the sender. New subscribers, long-term customers, and inactive contacts may respond differently. Decide how the platform will split the audience and how the winning version will be selected before the campaign starts.

4. Review more than opens

Open reporting can be affected by privacy features and image loading. Use it as one signal, not the full decision. Review clicks, conversions relevant to the email, unsubscribes, complaints, delivery problems, and replies. A subject that attracts curiosity but creates disappointment is not a useful winner.

5. Protect sender trust

Gmail's sender guidance emphasizes accurate sender information and non-deceptive subject lines. Do not use an emoji or unusual character to imitate a system graphic or suggest verification. Authentication, consent, list quality, sending consistency, and an easy unsubscribe process matter far more than visual decoration.

6. Allow enough data, then stop the test

A very small list can produce a dramatic percentage difference from only a few people. Do not declare a universal rule from one campaign. Use the testing method available in your email platform, document the audience and versions, and repeat only when you have a new question worth testing.

  1. Write the plain subject first.
  2. Add one relevant emoji without changing the promise.
  3. Preview both versions on desktop and mobile inboxes.
  4. Run the platform's controlled split test.
  5. Review engagement and negative signals together.
  6. Record the result as audience-specific evidence, not a permanent law.

7. Keep the email itself consistent with the subject

If the subject mentions delivery, the email should lead with the delivery update. If it promises a guide, the guide should be easy to reach. A subject-line test cannot repair a message that lacks a clear purpose or useful content.
