How to Use Emojis in Email Subject Lines and Newsletters

Written by Bavum, Builder and Editor at Mojitap · Originally published June 2026 · Updated August 7, 2026

An email emoji should clarify tone or category, not imitate a verification badge, hide the sender, or replace important words. Keep the subject accurate, use the preview text for additional context, and test only when you have enough recipients and a clear success measure.

Best default

Use zero or one emoji in a subject line. Put the specific topic first when the beginning of the subject may be all a reader sees. Do not assume an emoji improves opens or delivery.

Write the accurate subject before adding an emoji

The subject should represent the actual content. Avoid false reply prefixes, manufactured urgency, or symbols that imply approval, security, or identity verification. Gmail's sender guidance specifically warns against misleading subject elements and deceptive uses of emoji or other non-standard characters.

Protect the important words from truncation

Email clients show different amounts of subject text. Put the event, product, deadline, or requested action before a decorative ending. A row of emojis at the beginning can consume the most visible space.

Topic hidden

🎉✨ A special update you do not want to miss...

Topic visible

August product update: three keyboard fixes ✨

Adjust the tone for the relationship

A newsletter subscriber, a colleague, and a person receiving an unsolicited business message have different expectations. In a first contact, clarity and relevance matter more than friendliness markers. Do not use an emoji to make an irrelevant message feel personal.

ContextSafer approachRisk to avoid
NewsletterUse one recurring category cue if it helps scanning.Changing symbols every send until the brand becomes inconsistent.
Transactional messageState the receipt, security action, or delivery status in words.Using a symbol that makes a routine notice look like an emergency.
Business introductionExplain why the message is relevant in the first sentence.Using a casual emoji to disguise a generic mass message.

Test without overreading the result

  1. Choose one subject and change only the emoji treatment.
  2. Keep sender, audience, offer, schedule, and body content the same.
  3. Define the primary measure before sending.
  4. Check whether the sample is large enough to support a decision.
  5. Record “no clear difference” when the result is inconclusive.

For a fuller experiment workflow, use the emoji and no-emoji testing guide.

Newsletter accessibility checks

  • Do not use a repeated emoji row as a section heading.
  • Give links descriptive text instead of “click here 👉”.
  • Keep important information in live text, not only in an image.
  • Read the subject aloud and check whether the emoji name interrupts it.
  • Make unsubscribe and sender information easy to find.
