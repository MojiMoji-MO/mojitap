How to Test Emojis in Email Subject Lines Without Guessing

Written by Bavum, Builder and Editor at Mojitap · Originally published June 2026 · Updated July 24, 2026 · About 9 minutes

An emoji in an email subject line can make the topic easier to recognize, but it can also look repetitive, unclear, or promotional when it does not match the message. There is no universal symbol that guarantees a higher open rate. The useful question is whether a specific audience responds better to a clear emoji version than to an equally clear text-only version.

The safest workflow is simple: write an accurate subject line, create one restrained alternative with a relevant emoji, test the two versions under comparable conditions, and keep the result only when the data and reader experience support it.

Important distinction: Emojis are a creative variable, not a deliverability fix. Sender authentication, consent, list quality, honest subject lines, unsubscribe handling, and useful content matter far more than decoration.

1. Write an honest subject line first

The subject should accurately describe the email. Do not use a warning symbol for a routine message, imply a reply with “Re:” when the email is not a reply, or create false urgency. Gmail's sender guidance specifically emphasizes that subject lines and other message elements should accurately represent the sender and content.

Clear: “Registration closes Friday: Spring workshop”

Clear with a relevant emoji: “📅 Registration closes Friday: Spring workshop”

Misleading: “🚨 FINAL WARNING” for an ordinary promotional reminder

2. Choose one function for the emoji

A subject-line emoji should have a specific purpose. It may identify a category, support the tone, or make a recurring series recognizable. If it does none of those things, it is probably unnecessary.

Purpose Example Review question
Event or date 📅 Workshop schedule for August Does the calendar clarify the topic without adding urgency?
Educational tip 💡 Three ways to simplify your weekly report Is the email actually instructional?
Delivery update 📦 Your order has shipped Is the status factual and expected?
Celebration 🎉 Welcome to the community Does the tone fit the relationship and brand?

3. Test one variable at a time

If one version changes the emoji, wording, offer, and send time at once, you will not know what caused the difference. Keep the sender name, email content, audience selection, timing, and offer as consistent as the platform allows. Change only the subject-line treatment you want to evaluate.

A basic test can use:

  • Version A: a clear text-only subject line;
  • Version B: the same subject line with one relevant emoji;
  • Primary review: opens or another platform-selected metric;
  • Secondary review: clicks, unsubscribes, spam complaints, and downstream behavior.

Email platforms such as Mailchimp support subject-line A/B tests and explicitly recommend testing what a particular audience prefers. A result from one list should not be presented as a universal rule for every industry or subscriber group.

4. Do not treat open rate as a complete answer

Open measurement can be affected by privacy features, image loading, filtering, and platform behavior. A winning subject line should also lead to useful engagement after the open. If an emoji increases curiosity but the email disappoints the reader, the apparent short-term result may not be valuable.

Review clicks, replies, conversions relevant to the email, unsubscribes, and complaint signals where available. The right measure depends on the campaign's actual purpose.

5. Preview across devices and inboxes

Subject lines can be truncated on narrow screens, and emoji artwork differs across platforms. Send a test email to common desktop and mobile inboxes. Confirm that the sender name, subject, preview text, and first part of the message still work together.

  1. Check the subject on a small phone screen.
  2. Confirm that the emoji does not replace an essential word.
  3. Review the preview text so it adds information rather than repeating the subject.
  4. Choose a more established emoji if a newer character fails to render.
  5. Read the subject aloud to check tone and clarity.

6. Accessibility and recognition

Screen readers may announce the emoji name. One relevant symbol is usually easier to understand than a row of decorative characters. Keep the meaningful words in the subject line and avoid using emoji as the only way to communicate urgency, status, or category.

The sender name should remain recognizable. Do not use an emoji or image to imply that a sender is verified, official, or endorsed when that is not true.

7. Deliverability depends on sender practices

An emoji count is not a reliable shortcut for deciding whether Gmail or another provider will place a message in spam. Deliverability is influenced by factors such as authentication, spam complaints, sending patterns, list quality, consent, content accuracy, and unsubscribe practices. Follow the requirements of the email provider and the laws that apply to your audience.

Avoid promising that a “safe” emoji will bypass filters. No emoji can replace SPF, DKIM, DMARC where required, one-click unsubscribe for applicable bulk messages, or honest sending practices.
