How to Use Emojis in Email Subject Lines and Newsletters

Written by Bavum, Builder and Editor at Mojitap · Originally published June 2026 · Updated July 27, 2026 · About 10 minutes

Email gives an emoji several possible jobs. It can label the topic in a subject line, separate sections in a newsletter, soften a short welcome message, or mark a real status such as delivery or registration. The same symbol becomes distracting when it replaces an important word, repeats information, or makes an ordinary message look urgent.

This guide focuses on writing and layout: where an emoji can help, where it should be removed, and how to keep the message understandable on different inboxes and devices. For a controlled experiment comparing emoji and text-only subject lines, use the separate email A/B testing guide.

Working rule: Write the full meaning in words first. Add one emoji only when it helps a reader identify the topic or scan the layout. The email should remain complete and accurate after the emoji is removed.

1. Start with the purpose of the email

A useful email has one primary purpose: confirm an order, invite someone to an event, share a weekly update, teach a practical skill, request a decision, or welcome a new subscriber. Choose the subject line and the first paragraph around that purpose before considering any visual element.

Clear purpose: “Your workshop registration is confirmed”

Optional visual label: “📅 Your workshop registration is confirmed”

Unclear: “🎉🔥 Big news inside!!!” when the email is a routine reminder

The first two examples tell the reader what happened. The calendar simply reinforces the event topic. The third example asks the reader to open the message without providing a useful expectation.

2. Use a subject-line emoji as a label, not a replacement

A subject line is often viewed beside the sender name and preview text on a narrow screen. One familiar emoji can help a recurring category stand out, especially when the same sender publishes several types of updates. It should not replace the words that communicate date, price, status, security, or required action.

3. Decide whether the emoji belongs at the beginning or end

A symbol at the beginning works like a category marker. It is useful for recurring series such as events, shipping updates, recipes, or weekly tips. A symbol at the end usually adds tone after the meaning has already been stated. Neither position is universally better.

  • Beginning: use when fast category recognition is the main benefit.
  • End: use when the words should receive attention first.
  • Middle: avoid interrupting a short subject unless the symbol replaces no essential word.
  • Several emojis: remove them unless each has a clear and different function.

Put the most informative words early because inboxes may cut off the end. Send a test to a phone and a desktop inbox rather than relying only on the editor preview.

4. Make the preview text add new information

Preview text should continue the subject, not repeat it with more symbols. If the subject says “📅 Workshop schedule for August,” the preview can state the location, start time, or registration deadline. Repeating “Workshop schedule for August 📅” wastes limited space.

Subject: 📦 Your order has shipped

Useful preview: Track the package and review the estimated delivery date.

Repetitive preview: Your package has shipped 📦

5. Use emojis inside a newsletter as navigation cues

In a longer newsletter, a small set of repeated visual labels can help readers move between sections. A book can mark reading recommendations, a calendar can mark events, and a tool can mark a practical task. The text heading must still identify the section because screen readers, translations, and different fonts may present the symbol differently.

Keep the cue vocabulary small. When the same emoji means “new,” “important,” “sale,” and “click here,” it stops helping. Use headings, spacing, and descriptive link text as the main structure; treat emojis as optional reinforcement.

6. Keep calls to action explicit

A button or link should say what happens next: “View the schedule,” “Track the package,” or “Read the full guide.” A pointing hand or arrow can draw attention, but a symbol alone does not give enough information. Avoid rows of arrows around every link because they compete with one another and make the page harder to scan.

7. Check accessibility, rendering, and tone

Screen readers can announce an emoji by name, and platforms draw the same Unicode character differently. Read the subject aloud, keep meaningful words intact, and test newer or complex emojis on the devices your audience commonly uses. For an important message, prefer a familiar symbol over a recently introduced character that may appear as a box on older software.

  1. Confirm that the message works without the emoji.
  2. Check the sender name, subject, and preview text together.
  3. Open the email on at least one small screen.
  4. Listen with a screen reader when emoji use is frequent or structural.
  5. Remove the symbol when its tone could be read as sarcastic, urgent, or misleading.

The broader emoji accessibility guide explains how repeated symbols and decorative sequences can affect spoken output.

8. Sender trust matters more than decoration

An emoji is not a deliverability shortcut. Accurate sender information, consent, list quality, authentication, useful content, complaint rates, and unsubscribe handling are more important. Do not imitate a verification badge, security warning, reply marker, or system notification. Subject lines should represent the actual content of the message.
