How to Use Emojis in Email Subject Lines and Newsletters

Written by Bavum, Builder and Editor at Mojitap · Originally published June 2026 · Updated September 16, 2026

An email emoji belongs in a complete subject or sentence, not in place of the message's identity, deadline, or action. Begin with the text-only version. Add a relevant character only if the email remains clear when that character is hidden or shown differently. This applies to newsletters and ordinary business email; it is not a rule that every message needs an emoji.

The most common editing mistake is treating the sender name, subject, preview text, and body as one long advertising space. They do different jobs. The examples below use an imaginary workshop reminder so you can separate those jobs before touching the emoji picker.

Separate the four parts of an inbox entry

ElementUse it forExample
Sender display nameWho sent the messageOak Street Studio
SubjectThe particular event or reason for writingYour pottery workshop starts Saturday
Preview or preheader textThe useful detail following the subjectArrive at 9:50 AM; materials are provided.
Body and action linkInstructions, context, and an accurately named destinationView workshop details

For messages sent to personal Gmail accounts, Google's sender guidelines require display names to identify the sender rather than carry subject content. They also warn against names that use emoji to imitate graphic interface elements. A subject-line emoji is not a way to turn a sender name into a verification badge.

Edit a reminder without hiding the useful words

Before: 🔔🏺 IMPORTANT!!! Don't forget!!

Text first: Your pottery workshop starts Saturday

Optional emoji version: Your pottery workshop starts Saturday 🏺

Preview text: Arrive at 9:50 AM; materials are provided.

The last version does not become better merely because it has a symbol. It gives the sender two readable alternatives. On a narrow inbox, losing the final pottery symbol is less consequential than losing “Saturday” after a long decorative prefix.

No single character count guarantees the same visible subject on every phone and email program. Send yourself a draft and compare the sender, subject, and preview together. A good subject may still be undermined by preview text such as “View this email in your browser” when the actual reminder is below it.

Use different defaults for different email purposes

A casual newsletter: “Three pottery projects for a rainy weekend 🏺” tells readers what is inside. Keep the subject aligned with the actual number and type of projects.

A one-to-one request: “Could you review the attached workshop schedule?” usually needs no emoji. Add a character only when it suits the established conversation, not as a substitute for explaining what review is needed.

A cancellation: “Saturday workshop cancelled — next steps for participants” should not begin with a party icon carried over from the original invitation. Replace the wording and recheck any automatic emoji suggestions.

An order update: “Your order has shipped 📦” must mean shipment has actually happened. “Your order is being prepared” is a different state; a checkmark should not blur that distinction.

An invoice or security message: retain an identifiable sender, an explicit reason for the message, and clear actions. Do not imitate unread counts, warnings, or verified badges to make an ordinary promotional email look like an account alert.

Do not confuse an emoji reply with an email reaction

A character typed into the subject or body is part of the text. A Gmail or Outlook reaction is a separate feature attached to a received message and can appear differently outside the original email client. The insertion steps are covered in the Outlook guide; reaction availability and undo behavior belong in the Gmail reactions guide.

This distinction also helps when forwarding. A written “Approved for Saturday” carries explicit meaning in the message. A thumbs-up reaction may not explain what was approved or which version it referred to. Record the decision in words when the distinction matters.

Check the body, not just the subject

Read the draft once while ignoring every emoji. Can the recipient still find the appointment, the attachment description, and the next action? Then look at the action link: “View workshop details” is more useful than an isolated arrow. If you control the HTML, keep decorative emoji separate from the meaningful link label rather than relying on an unexplained symbol.

For a recurring newsletter, use a consistent optional marker for one recurring section, such as “Reading list 📚”. Do not assign the same symbol to unrelated sections merely to avoid writing section headings. This is an editing convention, not a claim about how inbox algorithms rank your message.

When the emoji version does not behave as expected

A box appears: compare the same character in another app and use an older, widely supported alternative if needed. The broken emoji guide separates unsupported characters from damaged pasted text.

It becomes an image: use a standard character from All Emojis instead of a sticker or GIF. A copied picture and an emoji character are not interchangeable.

The draft opens differently on a phone: review the full inbox row and the email body separately. Fix the missing or obscured information, not just the symbol's size.

You want to compare engagement: compare an otherwise identical text-only subject and emoji subject under a controlled experiment. Do not claim a causal improvement because a later newsletter performed differently. Use the emoji comparison worksheet to plan the metric and interpret small differences.

A finished email is one where the recipient can identify the sender, understand the reason for the message, and act without decoding an icon. An emoji may remain, but it should not be needed to recover those essentials.
